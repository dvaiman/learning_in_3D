A three-dimensional framework

Difficulty, time, and learning

An interactive model that integrates the challenge point framework, desirable difficulties, and cognitive load theory — with attention to the role of effort and AI-mediated practice.

The figure below is a discussion tool, not an exact model. It is meant to make explicit the trade-offs between how long we practise, how hard the practice is, and how much durable learning results. Rotate the plot, toggle the trajectories, and read the corresponding theory cards underneath.

Click a trajectory above to read the underlying theory. Click again to hide it.

Theoretical foundations

Six strands of learning science underpin the model. Each card connects a theory to a specific feature of the plot.

Surface

Challenge point framework

Guadagnoli & Lee (2004)

The optimal surface in the plot. The level of difficulty that maximises learning depends on the learner's current skill. As skill grows over time, the optimal difficulty must rise to maintain a productive challenge.

“Practising a skill does not necessarily guarantee skill learning.” The right level of functional difficulty is required.
Trajectory A

Desirable difficulties

Bjork (1994); Bjork & Bjork (2011)

Four strategies that make learning harder in the moment yet better in the long run: spacing, interleaving, retrieval practice, and generation. They tend to feel inefficient while practising but produce stronger retention and transfer.

What feels slow and effortful during learning is often what produces durable learning — a counter-intuitive principle.
Time axis

Spacing effect

Bjork & Allen (1970); Cepeda et al. (2006); Murphy, Bjork & Bjork (2023)

Distributed practice yields markedly better retention than massed practice. Murphy and colleagues showed that even within a fixed amount of study time, short repeated exposures outperform long single sessions.

Time is not only a quantity; it has a qualitative dimension. How time is distributed matters as much as how much time is spent.
Trajectory C

Cognitive load theory

Sweller (1988); Sweller, van Merriënboer & Paas (1998)

When the total cognitive load — intrinsic, extraneous, and germane — exceeds working-memory capacity, learning collapses. In the plot this appears as the orange trajectory: difficulty too high, too fast, with the learning curve bending downward.

Beyond a certain point, difficulty stops being “desirable” and becomes overwhelming.
Trajectory D

Cognitive offloading & the fluency illusion

Gerlich (2025); Bastani et al. (PNAS); Bjork & Bjork (2011)

AI tools reduce the cognitive effort of a task, which can produce an experience of efficient learning without the deep processing that supports retention. Bastani et al. found that students practising with AI performed 48% better during practice but 17% worse without it — a classic fluency illusion.

The very tool that could calibrate difficulty is instead used to eliminate it.
Optimal band

Zone of proximal development

Vygotsky (1978)

Converges with the challenge point framework: optimal learning takes place just beyond what the student can do alone. In the plot this corresponds to the narrow band around the optimal surface.

AI can act as the “more knowledgeable other” Vygotsky described — but only if it calibrates support rather than taking over the task.
